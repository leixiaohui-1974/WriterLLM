## Overall Assessment

This is my first review of this paper. The paper presents a Multi-Agent System (MAS) architecture and Water Systems Autonomy Levels (WSAL) classification for autonomous water networks, with two case studies. The paper addresses a genuinely important gap: the water sector lacks the formal architectural, safety, and maturity frameworks that automotive and aerospace have developed over decades. The three-component decomposition MAS = HDC + ODD + CI is clean and well-motivated. The WSAL classification with quantitative admission criteria is a practical contribution.

From a control-theoretic perspective, the MPC formulation (Equations 2–4) and DMPC extension (Equation 5) are correctly stated. The IDZ internal model, the Padé + ZOH discretization pathway, and the reference to Litrico & Fromion (2004) and Schuurmans et al. (1995) are all standard and appropriate. The convergence caveat for non-convex/strongly-coupled DMPC is an important and honest qualification. The ODD definition (Definition 1) as a Cartesian product is mathematically clean and directly verifiable.

However, I have several concerns regarding mathematical precision and the gap between the paper's formal language and what is actually proven or verified. These are refinement issues, not structural problems.

**Score: 8/10**
**Verdict: Minor Revision**

## Major Issues

### M1. "Mathematically Provable Constraint Satisfaction" Claim

§3.1 concludes: "every control decision at every layer is computed from mechanistic models...with mathematically provable constraint satisfaction." This is a strong claim that is not substantiated in the paper. MPC constraint satisfaction depends on: (a) feasibility of the optimization at every time step, which requires a non-empty feasible set; (b) model accuracy—if the IDZ internal model deviates significantly from the physical system, the predicted constraint-satisfying trajectory may violate constraints when applied to the real system; and (c) recursive feasibility, which requires specific terminal constraint or terminal cost design (Mayne et al., 2000). None of these conditions are discussed.

**Recommendation**: Qualify the claim: "with mathematically provable constraint satisfaction under the assumption that the internal model accurately represents the controlled subsystem within the ODD and that the optimization is recursively feasible." Alternatively, add a sentence noting that recursive feasibility is ensured by the Layer 0 safety floor as a backup: even if the MPC solution is infeasible or constraint-violating due to model mismatch, Layer 0 catches the violation. This is actually a stronger argument.

### M2. Hierarchical Optimality and Consistency

The four-layer HDC architecture (§3.1) and the four-horizon integration in Jiaodong (§6.2) implement a hierarchical decomposition: Layer 3 computes reference trajectories that Layer 2 tracks, and Layer 2 computes references that Layer 1 tracks. This nested structure raises the standard question of hierarchical consistency: does the overall closed-loop system achieve the Layer 3 objective, or does the decomposition introduce suboptimality? More critically, can the reference trajectories generated by Layer 3 (using a steady-state model) actually be tracked by Layer 1 (using a dynamic model) within the constraints? If Layer 3 generates an infeasible reference, what happens?

**Recommendation**: Add a brief discussion acknowledging: (a) hierarchical decomposition introduces suboptimality relative to a hypothetical monolithic optimizer—this is the standard price of scalability; (b) the reference feasibility issue is addressed by the time-scale separation: Layer 3 operates at horizons (days-years) where transient dynamics are negligible, so steady-state references are dynamically achievable; (c) if a Layer 3 reference is temporarily infeasible at Layer 1 (e.g., due to a rapid demand change), the Layer 1 MPC handles this through constraint softening and the discrepancy propagates back to Layer 2/3 via the DMPC update cycle.

### M3. Feasibility Definition in Propose-Validate Loop

Equations (9)–(10) define the CI-HDC propose-validate interaction but do not specify what "feasibility" means formally. Does HDC check that: (a) the MPC optimization problem with the proposed parameters θ_new and constraints C_new has a non-empty feasible set? (b) the resulting trajectory satisfies all constraints (Equation 4)? (c) the closed-loop system is stable under the proposed parameters? These are different levels of feasibility with different computational costs.

**Recommendation**: Define feasibility explicitly: "HDC validates the CI proposal by solving the constrained optimization (Equations 2–4) with the proposed parameters (θ_new, y_ref_new, C_new) over a verification horizon N_v. The proposal is accepted if and only if the optimization returns a feasible solution satisfying all hard constraints (Equation 4). Stability is ensured by the recursive structure of the MPC formulation; computational tractability is maintained because the verification uses the same solver as the operational MPC."

## Minor Issues

### m1. Padé Order Specification
The paper states that the state-space matrices are derived through "Padé approximation and zero-order hold discretization" but does not specify the Padé order. For the IDZ model with delay, a first-order or second-order Padé approximation is typical, but the choice affects closed-loop stability margins, particularly for systems with long delays (like Jiaodong with transport delays of hours). State the order used.

### m2. τ_coast Wave Celerity Values
Equation (8) uses c₀ ≈ 20 m/s for Shaping and c₀ ≈ 1 m/s for Jiaodong. For a reservoir-river system, 20 m/s is reasonable as a pressure wave celerity in a penstock. For an open channel, the gravity wave celerity is c₀ = √(gA/B) where A is cross-sectional area and B is top width; for a typical trapezoidal canal with 2–3 m depth, this gives c₀ ≈ 4–5 m/s, not 1 m/s. The value of 1 m/s appears to represent a kinematic wave speed rather than a dynamic wave speed. Clarify which wave speed is being used and why—the choice significantly affects τ_coast.

### m3. Equation (6) — Not Really an Equation
Equation (6) specifies the Layer 3 output format rather than a mathematical relationship. Consider labeling it as a "Definition" or "Protocol" rather than numbering it as an equation, or add a brief optimization formulation showing what Layer 3 actually solves.

### m4. DMPC Communication Topology
The DMPC formulation (Equation 5) assumes a serial chain topology (agent i communicates with i−1 and i+1). This is appropriate for the Jiaodong canal but may not apply to the Shaping cascade where all stages are hydraulically connected through a river. Clarify whether the Shaping DMPC uses the same serial topology or a more connected graph.

### m5. Terminal Cost/Constraint in MPC
The MPC formulation (Equation 2) uses a finite-horizon cost without a terminal cost term or terminal constraint set. For guaranteed closed-loop stability, standard MPC theory requires either a terminal cost V_f(x(k+N)) or a terminal constraint x(k+N) ∈ X_f (Mayne et al., 2000). For a JWRPM audience this may not be critical, but adding a brief note that "in practice, the receding-horizon implementation with Layer 0 backup provides robust constraint satisfaction even without formal terminal conditions" would be appropriate.

## Summary

The paper makes a genuine contribution to the water engineering literature by providing a formal architecture and maturity classification that the water sector currently lacks. The v03→v04 revisions (digital twin positioning, human factors, resilience, abstract condensation) have improved the cross-disciplinary breadth. The remaining issues are primarily about mathematical precision: qualifying constraint satisfaction claims, clarifying hierarchical optimality, and defining feasibility in the propose-validate loop. These are refinement issues that can be addressed in a single revision round. The core framework is sound and the two-case validation design is well-conceived.
